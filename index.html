Text. Model of Communication in Personnel Management

Within any organisation, the flow of information between people follows a process that specialists in personnel management need to understand in some depth. Communication can be defined as an exchange in which one participant, the source, formulates and transmits a message, while another, the receiver, receives and interprets it. Before a message can travel from one person to another, the source must encode it, translating an idea into words, gestures or symbols; the receiver must then decode it, extracting meaning from what has arrived. The information that a receiver subsequently returns to the source, whether a nod of agreement or a written response to an appraisal, is known as feedback, and it is feedback more than anything else that allows a manager to judge whether an instruction has genuinely been understood.

Messages exchanged inside an organisation may be verbal, delivered through speech or writing, or nonverbal, conveyed through gesture, posture, tone of voice or facial expression. A candidate's handshake at the start of an interview, a manager's folded arms during a difficult conversation, or the layout of an open-plan office can all communicate something before a single word is spoken, and an alert HR professional learns to read these signals as carefully as any written policy.

Every message travels through a particular channel: a face-to-face conversation relies on a vocal and visual channel simultaneously, whereas a company-wide memo relies on a written, often digital, channel. The choice of channel is rarely neutral. An announcement about redundancies delivered by group email, rather than in a private meeting, communicates something about an organisation quite independently of the words the email actually contains.

Communication never occurs in a vacuum: it always takes place within a context. The physical context is the literal setting of an exchange, an open-plan office or a closed meeting room; the social-psychological context comprises the organisational hierarchy and the relationships between the people involved, since an instruction from a direct line manager lands very differently from the same words spoken by a stranger from another department; and the temporal context refers to the timing of an exchange, since an appraisal held the day after a difficult quarter is received quite differently from the same appraisal held during a period of high morale.

Communication tends to be effective when participants share a comparable body of experience. A newly onboarded employee, for instance, may struggle to interpret an instruction that relies entirely on internal jargon or unwritten rules that longer-serving colleagues take for granted; genuine understanding, in such cases, depends on a conscious effort from both sides to close that gap.

Communication invariably produces some effect on the people who take part in it, whether a change in what they know, how they feel, or how they behave at work. When any form of interference, generally referred to as noise, enters this system, communication becomes less effective. Noise may be physical, as when a poorly worded memo is genuinely hard to read; psychological, as when personal bias colours the way a disciplinary letter is interpreted; or semantic, as when a lack of shared vocabulary, for instance around unfamiliar HR terminology, prevents two people from understanding each other at all.

4. Grammar in Use

This unit's grammar strand starts with the English tense system, since the topic of communication constantly requires you to talk about habits, single events, ongoing situations and plans. Rules are given in small portions, one or two tenses at a time, and each rule is followed immediately by an exercise. This is exercise 1 of 10 in the current installment; further exercises (modals, conditionals, reported speech) will build on these in later units.

4.1 Present Simple and Present Continuous

Rule. Present Simple: habits, facts, routines, permanent situations. Signal words: usually, often, always, every day, rarely, never. Present Continuous: an action happening now or around now, a temporary situation, a changing situation. Signal words: now, at the moment, currently, these days, look/listen.

8. Writing

This unit's writing strand does not ask you to produce a finished piece of writing yet. Instead, it builds the two most basic skills a writer needs: recognising the sentence that carries the main idea of a paragraph, and using punctuation correctly.

8.1 The topic sentence

Rule. A topic sentence states the main idea of a paragraph; the other sentences support it with detail, example or explanation. In English academic writing, the topic sentence is usually, though not always, the first sentence of the paragraph.
Exercise 1. Identify the topic sentence

8.2 Punctuation: commas

Rule. Use a comma: (1) between items in a list of three or more; (2) before a coordinating conjunction (and, but, so, or) that joins two complete sentences; (3) after an introductory word or phrase (however, for example, at the meeting); (4) around extra, non-essential information in the middle of a sentence.
